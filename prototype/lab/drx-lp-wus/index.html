Listening costs battery.
Sleeping costs delay.

A connected phone has to keep checking whether the network has something for it, and that checking is what drains the radio. Discontinuous reception lets it sleep on an agreed schedule. A low-power wake-up signal lets it sleep until called. Send the phone some data and see when it actually arrives.

The whole story, from zero

  1. Listening is the expensive part

    The base station (gNB) announces every transmission on a control channel. A phone that might receive something has to decode that channel every slot. Nearly always the answer is nothing for you, and the radio was powered for it anyway.

  2. DRX is an agreed sleeping schedule

    With discontinuous reception the phone wakes at the start of every cycle and listens for a short on-duration. The gNB knows the schedule, so it holds data for the phone until the next on-duration begins.

  3. Data keeps the phone up

    When something does arrive, more usually follows. Each new transmission restarts an inactivity timer, and the phone keeps listening until that timer runs out.

  4. Awake means any timer is running

    The phone listens whenever the on-duration timer or the inactivity timer is running. The specification calls this Active Time and adds a few more cases, such as waiting for a retransmission or for the answer to a scheduling request.

  5. A second, tiny receiver

    Even an empty on-duration costs a wake-up. A low-power wake-up receiver draws very little and listens only for one simple signal, the LP-WUS. While it listens, the main radio can stay asleep.

  6. Two ways to use it

    Together with the DRX cycle: before each on-duration the phone checks for an LP-WUS, and starts the on-duration only if it was called. Or on its own clock: the on-duration is not started at all, and an LP-WUS starts a separate monitoring timer whenever data shows up.

What each number is for

The gNB picks these per phone and sends them by RRC. Every one of them moves the same two dials in opposite directions.

The question
The knob
The trade
How often does the phone check in?
drx-LongCycle
The longest a packet can wait is about one cycle. A long cycle sleeps more and delays more.
How long does it listen each time?
drx-onDurationTimer
Long gives the gNB room to schedule many phones that wake together. Every millisecond of it is paid on every cycle, data or not.
After data, how long does it stay up?
drx-InactivityTimer
Long keeps a conversation flowing without waiting for the next cycle. Set longer than the gap between packets, the phone never sleeps at all.
Should an empty on-duration be skipped?
LP-WUS monitoring, without lpwus-PDCCH-MonitoringTimer
The phone wakes only for cycles where it was called. Delay stays tied to the DRX cycle. If it could not listen for the signal, it starts the on-duration to be safe.
Should waking follow the data instead of the cycle?
lpwus-PDCCH-MonitoringTimer
The on-duration is not started. An LP-WUS starts this timer and the phone listens while it runs. Delay now depends on how often the wake-up receiver gets an occasion, not on the DRX cycle.

What this lab simplifies. Downlink only, one phone, one cell, one packet delivered per millisecond while awake. Energy is in made-up relative units: awake 100 per ms, asleep 1, wake-up receiver 1 more while the main radio sleeps, and 200 for each wake-up; real figures depend on the device and on how deeply it can sleep. With LP-WUS before the cycle, the occasion sits 3 ms ahead of the on-duration; on its own clock, the main radio starts listening 2 ms after the signal. Not modelled: the short DRX cycle, DRX command MAC CEs, HARQ round-trip and retransmission timers, uplink and scheduling requests (which also wake the phone), the older PDCCH-based wake-up indication (DCP), CSI reporting rules, two DRX groups, and LP-WUS in idle mode.
