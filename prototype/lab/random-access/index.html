    The whole story, from zero

    1. Why knock at all

      Normally a phone sends only when the base station (gNB) grants it a slot. A phone that is new to the cell, or has been idle, has no way to ask. The cell therefore sets aside regular moments, called random access occasions, where anyone may transmit without permission.

    2. Message 1: the knock

      The phone picks one of up to 64 short signals, called preambles, at random and sends it. A preamble carries no name and no data. It only says someone is here, on this door.

    3. If nobody answers, knock louder

      The phone starts quietly so it does not drown out others. If no reply comes within a set window, it cannot know whether it was too quiet or collided. It raises its power one step, waits a random time, and tries again.

    4. Message 2: the answer to a door

      The gNB replies to the door, not to a person: timing correction, a small uplink grant and a temporary identity. Every phone that used that door hears the same answer and believes it is theirs.

    5. Message 3: say who you are

      The phone uses the grant to send its identity. If two phones shared the door, both transmit on the same grant, on top of each other.

    6. Message 4: the gNB names one winner

      The gNB echoes back the identity it managed to decode. A phone that hears its own identity is in. A phone that hears another, or nothing before a timer runs out, has lost and starts again from message 1.

    Two shortcuts exist. When the gNB already knows the phone, as in a handover or a beam recovery, it can reserve a door for it, so no collision is possible and messages 3 and 4 are not needed. A 2-step variant sends the knock and the identity together.

    What each number is for

    The gNB broadcasts most of these to every phone in the cell. One of them, the backoff, it can change from moment to moment inside message 2.

    The question
    The knob
    The trade
    How many doors are there?
    totalNumberOfRA-Preambles
    up to 64 per occasion, shared between beams and reserved uses
    More doors mean fewer collisions. Each door kept for contention is one that cannot be reserved for handovers and beam recovery.
    How loud is the first knock, and each one after?
    preambleReceivedTargetPower, powerRampingStep
    step of 0, 2, 4 or 6 dB
    A low start protects other users from interference but costs extra attempts. A big step gets heard sooner and overshoots more.
    How long to wait for an answer?
    ra-ResponseWindow
    1 to 80 slots
    Short retries quickly. Long gives a busy gNB time to answer, and makes every failed attempt slower.
    After a failure, how long to stay away?
    Backoff Indicator
    sent in message 2; 5 ms to 1,920 ms
    Each phone waits a random time up to this value. A long backoff spreads a crowd out and cuts collisions. It also makes every retry slower, even in an empty cell.
    How long to wait for the verdict?
    ra-ContentionResolutionTimer
    8 to 64 subframes
    If message 4 has not named this phone when the timer ends, the attempt has failed.
    When to stop trying?
    preambleTransMax
    3 to 200 attempts
    Past this count MAC reports a random access problem upward, and the phone treats the link as failed. Low gives up early in a crowd. High keeps a hopeless phone transmitting.

    What this lab simplifies. Contention-based, 4-step random access only, on one beam. Sixteen doors in the first part so they fit on screen. When phones share a door, the gNB decodes one of them half the time and nobody the other half; real outcomes depend on received power and timing. Whether the first knock is loud enough is a hidden random level, not a path-loss calculation, and each attempt adds 2 dB. A failed attempt costs a fixed 40 ms before the backoff starts; a successful one takes 30 ms. Occasions come every 10 ms. Not modelled: beam (SSB) selection and its mapping to occasions, preamble groups A and B, the response window and contention timer as separate clocks, HARQ on message 3, contention-free and 2-step random access, prioritised access.
